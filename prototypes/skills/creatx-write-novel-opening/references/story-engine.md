# Story Engine Reference

## Choose story before arranging lore

Treat the world as a source of pressure, places, people, imagery, and consequences. Choose a recognizable narrative engine before deciding which lore files deserve screen time.

Useful mature archetypes include:

- **Monster hunt:** a creature threatens a community; denial or delay worsens the danger; a bounded group must find and confront it.
- **Quest or reclamation:** a reluctant or unlikely protagonist joins a journey to reach, recover, or restore something lost.
- **Community defense:** a vulnerable settlement gathers mismatched defenders before a known attack.
- **Rescue or escape:** people are trapped behind a closing route, hostile territory, disaster, siege, or social boundary.
- **Heist:** a team with complementary skills must obtain one guarded object under time pressure.
- **Revenge or reckoning:** a past injury creates a concrete target, while pursuit changes what justice would mean.
- **Forbidden bond:** two people whose loyalties cannot coexist attempt to preserve a relationship under a visible deadline.

Borrow the engine, not another work's characters, scenes, language, or signature invention.

## Minimum story engine

Complete this sentence in natural language:

> The protagonist must accomplish **X** before **Y**, otherwise they lose **Z**, because **Z** has an irreplaceable personal meaning.

Reject goals such as “become stronger,” “find freedom,” “save the world,” “investigate the truth,” or “solve the crisis” until success and failure can be observed.

Answer four questions:

1. What does the protagonist want?
2. Why must they get it?
3. By when?
4. What do they lose if they fail?

## Three goal layers

- **Long-term goal:** the observable endpoint of the story.
- **Stage goal:** the complete result pursued during the current movement, act, or volume.
- **Immediate goal:** what the protagonist must do now or during the next few chapters.

The immediate goal creates action. The stage goal creates a satisfying section. The long-term goal tells the reader where the story is going.

## Goal versus character

Choose a goal that pressures a character's defended trait or avoidance. Do not merely assign errands.

Examples:

- A cautious observer must decide before all evidence is available.
- A loyal soldier must choose which incompatible oath to break.
- A charming liar must ask someone to believe an unembellished truth.
- A solitary expert must entrust survival to people they consider unreliable.

Do not erase the character's strength. Make them learn its limit and accept responsibility for using it.

## Progress test

Keep a scene goal only if its success or failure changes at least one item:

- capability or resource;
- known truth;
- relationship;
- situation or position;
- a decision that cannot be fully taken back.

If the characters complete a task and remain in the same practical, relational, and cognitive position, remove or combine it.

## Two-chapter test

Chapter one should make the reader able to state:

- who acts;
- what they want now;
- why delay matters;
- what first event makes ordinary life impossible to resume unchanged.

Chapter two should:

- force commitment rather than further orientation;
- demonstrate the danger or promise in physical action;
- change the available explanation or route;
- end with a new necessity, discovery, threat, or decision.

Do not spend two chapters introducing factions before the protagonist begins pursuing the immediate goal.

## Anti-report pass

Before delivery, search for these symptoms:

- paragraphs that summarize institutions instead of dramatizing conflict;
- dialogue where every speaker explains the world correctly;
- repeated evidence, permission, responsibility, contract, classification, or procedure vocabulary without a personal consequence;
- lists disguised as prose;
- a protagonist who only observes while supporting characters make all decisions;
- cliffhangers created only by withholding information the viewpoint character already knows;
- ornamental maxims at the end of every scene.

Rewrite the affected passage as an action, confrontation, sensory discovery, choice, or loss.
